Recording Law

Can I record conversations?

Recording laws vary by state. One-party consent means you can record a conversation you're part of without telling the other person. All-party (two-party) consent means everyone must agree. Select your state.

Pattern Recognition

Recognizing coercive control

Check any behaviors you have experienced. This list is private — it is not saved or sent anywhere. It is here to help you name what you may be experiencing, not to diagnose your situation.
  • Isolation
    They limit or monitor contact with friends, family, or coworkers — or make you feel guilty for spending time with others.
  • Isolation
    They create conflict with people close to you, or spread misinformation to isolate you from your support network.
  • Surveillance
    They monitor your phone, messages, email, or location — either directly or through apps or devices you didn't consent to.
  • Surveillance
    They demand to know your location at all times, or show up unexpectedly to check on you.
  • Financial control
    They control access to money, make you account for every purchase, or prevent you from working or maintaining independent finances.
  • Financial control
    They have run up debt in your name, damaged your credit, or interfered with your employment.
  • Degradation
    They consistently criticize, humiliate, or dismiss you — in private or in front of others — in ways that undermine your self-worth.
  • Degradation
    They deny or minimize things they have done, accuse you of misremembering, or make you feel like your perception of events is unreliable.
  • Threats & intimidation
    They threaten harm to you, your children, pets, family members, or themselves if you don't comply with their demands.
  • Threats & intimidation
    They display weapons, destroy property, or use physical gestures to intimidate without direct contact.
  • Control of daily life
    They dictate what you wear, eat, who you can speak to, where you can go, or how you spend your time.
  • Control of daily life
    You feel you need to ask permission for ordinary activities, or that you modify your behavior to avoid their reaction.
  • Custody & children
    They use children as leverage — threatening custody, involving children in conflicts, or undermining your parental role.
  • Identity-based coercion
    They threaten to out your sexual orientation, gender identity, or immigration status as a form of control or punishment.
  • Reproductive coercion
    They control decisions about pregnancy, contraception, or reproductive healthcare without your agreement.
  • Technology & privacy
    They share intimate images without consent, use technology to humiliate you, or exploit your digital accounts or information.
  • Post-separation
    After separation, they use legal processes, shared children, finances, or mutual contacts to continue monitoring or controlling your life.
  • Gut feeling
    You feel afraid of their reactions, walk on eggshells, or have changed who you are because of this relationship — even if you can't point to a single incident.

Coercive control is a pattern of behaviors, not a single event. Any number of checks may be meaningful. Trust your experience.
Safety Planning

Planning for safety

A safety plan is a practical guide you create for yourself. There is no single right plan — it depends on your situation. These five steps cover what most safety plans address.

01
Identify danger signals

Note the warning signs that indicate a situation may escalate — specific words, behaviors, times of day, or triggers. Knowing these lets you act earlier.

  • What has happened just before past incidents?
  • Are there patterns around alcohol, stress, or specific topics?
  • What does your body feel before things escalate?
02
Know your exit

If you need to leave quickly, having a mental map reduces the time needed. You don't need to leave permanently — this is about having options.

  • Which exits are accessible without passing the person?
  • Where would you go? (Friend, family member, shelter, hotel?)
  • Do you have your keys, phone, ID within reach?
  • Is there a code word you can use with a trusted person?
03
Gather essential documents

Having copies of key documents stored separately (with a trusted person or in a secure cloud account) reduces barriers if you need to leave.

  • ID, passport, Social Security card or equivalent
  • Financial records, bank account numbers
  • Children's documents (birth certificates, school records, medical cards)
  • Medications you take regularly
  • Lease or mortgage documents; proof of ownership
04
Secure your communication

If your device or accounts may be monitored, use a device the person doesn't know about or a private browser session. Delete this page from your history after visiting if needed.

  • Signal (encrypted, disappearing messages) on a separate device
  • New email account created on a safe network
  • Library computers for sensitive research
  • Use incognito/private mode and clear history
05
Build your support network

Isolation is a tool of coercive control. Naming even one person you trust — and letting them know what's happening — matters.

  • One trusted person who can receive a code word or call
  • A local domestic violence organization (see Resources below)
  • If you have children, a trusted person who can take them in an emergency
  • National DV Hotline: 1-800-799-7233 (24/7, confidential)

Your Community

Specific protections and considerations

Coercive control intersects with identity in ways that affect both experience and legal options.

LGBTQ+ Survivors

Coercive control in same-sex and queer relationships is equally serious but often underrecognized by systems. Threatening to out a partner is a recognized form of coercion. LGBTQ-specific shelters and legal advocates exist in most major cities.

LGBTQ+ resources →
Immigration Status

VAWA (Violence Against Women Act) allows undocumented survivors to self-petition for immigration status without relying on an abusive citizen or resident spouse. Law enforcement is not required to ask about your status when responding to DV calls.

VAWA immigration guide →
Language Access

The DV Hotline offers interpretation in more than 200 languages. Many local shelters have staff who speak languages other than English. You have the right to an interpreter in court proceedings at no cost.

Hotline: 1-800-799-7233 →
Disability

People with disabilities experience DV at higher rates and face additional barriers — caregiving dependency, accessible shelter scarcity, and communication access. Disability-specific advocates exist and ADA protections apply to shelter access.

Disability DV resources →
